> **TL;DR** — Running one project well isn't enough — sustained impact in people analytics comes from three ongoing habits: watching what peer organizations do, sharing your findings and methods internally, and actively soliciting feedback after every analysis.

---

## Intuition

The [[people-analytics-project-framework|project framework]] gets you through one cycle well. But expertise and organizational trust don't come from any single project — they compound only if you also look outward (what's working elsewhere), share inward (so an insight doesn't stay locked in your head), and close the loop (feedback sharpens the next cycle). Skip these three and every project stays a one-off instead of becoming a growing practice.

## Mechanics

**Three compounding habits, applied after or alongside every project:**

| Practice | What it means | Why it compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Monitor peer organizations | Track what methods and practices other organizations apply in people analytics | Keeps your methodology current; surfaces techniques you haven't tried yet |
| Share findings & methodology | Communicate results *and how you got them* to colleagues and other areas | Spreads the insight past your own head; builds organizational trust in the function |
| Solicit feedback | After sharing, actively ask for input on the analysis itself | Surfaces blind spots and new angles; directly improves the next project |

**This sits above the single-project framework, not inside it** — [[people-analytics-project-framework]]'s step 8 ("communicate results") is where sharing plugs into *one* project. This note treats sharing and feedback as a recurring habit *across* projects, not a one-time deliverable that ends when the deck is presented.

**Templates and structured guides are a means, not the practice** — a step-by-step template can carry you from phenomenon definition to a final presentation, but it doesn't substitute for the ongoing habits above; a well-templated project that's never shared or benchmarked externally still stays a one-off.

## In ML

**Peer monitoring is staying current with the field** — the ML equivalent is tracking papers and benchmarks; a methodology that's never compared to what else exists quietly goes stale even while it still technically works.

**Sharing methodology is documentation and reproducibility** — an insight that isn't documented and shared is the organizational equivalent of an undocumented model: nobody but its author can maintain, question, or extend it, and it dies when they leave the team.

**Feedback loops mirror the ML production cycle** — a deployed model needs monitoring and retraining from real-world feedback; a people analytics practice needs the same loop at the team level. Exposing findings, collecting feedback, and refining the next iteration is the human-scale version of train → deploy → monitor → retrain.

## Exercises

**Basic** — You finish a turnover analysis and present it only to your direct manager. Which of the three practices did you skip, and what's the concrete risk of skipping it?

**Intermediate** — Two teams at different companies independently solve a similar attrition problem, six months apart, with no shared learning between them. Which practice, applied earlier, could have shortened the second team's timeline, and how?

**Advanced** — Design a lightweight recurring ritual (e.g. monthly) that operationalizes all three practices for a 3-person people analytics team without adding significant overhead. Justify each ritual element against the specific practice it serves.
